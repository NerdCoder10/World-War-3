# World War 3 — Theatre Command

An interactive war-games map of **196 countries**: the 195 recognised states (193 UN members + Vatican City + Palestine) plus Taiwan. Each country has a dossier card with its GDP, military inventory, estimated "secret assets" and soft power. You play a campaign as one of a handful of unlocked nations.

## Run it

**Play online:** https://nerdcoder10.github.io/World-War-3/

No build step and no install needed. Open `index.html` in a browser. Everything except the web fonts is vendored, so it also works offline. You can also serve the folder, for example with `python3 -m http.server`.

## What's in it

**Satellite terrain.** The map is drawn over NASA's Blue Marble imagery (topography and sea-floor relief, public domain), warped onto the game's Natural Earth projection. The world view uses a 4,096-pixel image. When you zoom in, the visible area is redrawn at screen resolution from detail tiles: a 10,800-pixel world for country views and a 21,600-pixel world (about 1 km per pixel) for state, province and base views. Country colours turn translucent so the land shows through. Zoomed views stream aerial and satellite photos from Esri World Imagery (sub-metre in most populated areas; credit: Esri, Maxar, Earthstar Geographics and the GIS User Community), warped onto the map's projection. At base zoom you can see piers, runways, hangars and ships. The map zooms to 30,000× (about 1 m per screen pixel), and clicking a base flies in to about 6,000×. If Esri can't be reached, Sentinel-2 cloudless 2016 (EOX, CC BY 4.0) is used instead, and offline the bundled NASA terrain stays. The ⛰ button next to the zoom controls switches the terrain off. The images are in `vendor/terrain/`.

**Map layers** (top bar): Campaign (playable, locked and conquered), Military power, Secret assets, GDP, Soft power, National value. You can drag to pan, scroll to zoom, and click any country. Microstates and small islands get dot markers.

**Country card ("Dossier")**
- **National value**: the country's estimated total worth and its share of the world's value, split into human capital, real estate and land, business equity, cultural and brand capital, infrastructure, natural resources, gold and military hardware, plus value per citizen.
- **Force disposition**: a best guess at where the military is stationed in peacetime: the capital reserve, each border sized by how tense it is, and the main naval bases. It shows troops, tanks, aircraft and vessels at each location, and the locations appear as rings on the map when you select the country.
- **Economy**: nominal GDP (2025 est.) and its world rank, share of world GDP, GDP per capita, population.
- **Military power**: a 0–100 power index and rank, plus the defence budget, active and reserve troops, tanks, armoured vehicles, artillery, aircraft, fighters, helicopters, naval vessels, submarines, carriers and nuclear warheads.
- **Secret assets**: an estimated true-strength index, the estimated real defence spend including off-book money, gold reserves, sovereign wealth funds, 0–10 meters for intelligence, offensive cyber, nuclear capability/latency and proxies/irregulars, and an analyst note on what is believed to be hidden.
- **Soft power**: a 0–100 score, rank, tier and headline cultural assets.

**Rankings**: all 196 countries sorted by any metric, with a region filter.

## Locking rules

Every new campaign unlocks:
- 4 random African countries (South Africa is never picked)
- 3 random European countries
- 3 South American countries: Argentina always, plus 2 random (Brazil is never picked)
- Mexico, Mongolia, Kazakhstan and Bangladesh (always)

**Buying countries.** You can buy any country for its national value in coins, at 1 coin = $1. Before a campaign starts, buying a locked country makes it playable. During a campaign, buying a country adds it to your empire peacefully, and it never revolts. It arrives with troops at access level 1 and its weapons at level 0. The 50 most valuable countries cost extra: ranks 1–5 +132.5% (a 50% markup, then raised another 55%), 6–10 +40%, 11–20 +30%, 21–35 +15% and 36–50 +5%. For example, the United States costs about 1.54 quadrillion coins and Tuvalu about 1.5 billion.

All other countries, including Taiwan, are locked: you can't play as them, but you can inspect, invade or court them. Before you choose a nation you can reroll the random picks. The campaign is saved in your browser's localStorage.

## Campaign rules

The game runs in turns. Give as many orders as you like, then press **End turn** in the top bar: armies move, every battle fights one round, revolts are fought, forces regrow and you collect coins.

- **Armies**: open any country's dossier and use *Send an invasion force* (or *Move forces here* for your own nations). Choose where the units come from, either one of your nations' home forces or an army already in the field, and type exactly how many troops, tanks, armoured vehicles, artillery, aircraft, fighters, helicopters, ships, submarines and carriers to send. Only the share of each unit type that the nation's access levels allow can be sent (see below). Armies take time to arrive. Ground units march overland if the target borders the source nation, or borders your territory connected to it by land. Otherwise they go by sea, which needs enough sealift from ships (2 each), carriers (40) and transport aircraft (0.5) in the same army: each 1,000 troops needs 1, and each tank 0.05. Air and naval-only forces arrive in 1 turn. You can reinforce, redirect (send an army on from where it stands) or recall armies at any time, and armies that arrive home go back into reserve.
- **Battles** are fought one round per turn. Your army's strength (unit counts × combat value × equipment quality × weapons tech × trait bonuses) is set against the defender's remaining forces, which get a 25% home advantage, a morale bonus from soft power, their defensive trait and help from free treaty allies (NATO, CSTO, the US Pacific treaties, China–DPRK, Gulf Peninsula Shield), up to triple strength. Both sides take losses in proportion to the odds. You conquer a country once 80% of its pre-war forces are destroyed, and half the survivors join you with their weapons locked. If your army is wiped out, the invasion fails. A nuclear power may strike your armies once per war before it falls. Defenders not at war regrow 5% of their forces a turn, and your nations regrow 3%.
- **Diplomatic union**: peaceful annexation, tried once per country per turn. The odds depend on your soft power, GDP and strength compared with the target's. Nuclear powers and countries you are at war with can't be absorbed this way.

**Tiered access levels.** Every upgrade runs from level 1 to 100. Each weapon category has an access level for every nation you control: troops, tanks, armoured vehicles, artillery, aircraft, fighters, helicopters, naval vessels, submarines and carriers. Level 1 gives access to 10% of that category, rising linearly to 100% at level 90 (each level adds about 1%). Levels 91–100 are surge levels: access stays at 100% and each adds +1% combat power, up to +10%. Access is a ceiling on what you can call on, not what every attack must use: you still choose how many units to send. A nation's accessible share applies to its whole force (home plus deployed), so units already sent count against it. Defence, revolts and diplomacy use the accessible share too. Nations start with troops at level 1 and every weapon category at level 0 (locked). Nukes sit outside the levels and always count. A category's full price is 10 coins per $1bn of its estimated replacement value (minimum 25), and troops are priced at $50m per thousand. Level 1 costs 5% of that price, levels 2–90 about 2.1× it in total (each a little dearer than the last), and each surge level 5%. Each row in the dossier has +1, +10 and Max buttons, where Max buys as many levels as you can afford up to 90, then up to 100. You start with 300 coins. Each turn you earn 20 coins plus 2% of your empire's GDP in $bn, and each conquest or union pays 25 coins plus 5% of the target's GDP. Saves from before tiered access carry over: unlocked categories and troops become level 90, and each old technology level becomes 18 new levels.

**National traits (draft).** Every one of the 196 countries has its own trait, based on its geography, history or economy, for example Finland's *Sisu* (+30% defence), Mongolia's *Mongol Horde* (+16% invasion strength) and Norway's *Oil Fund* (+30 coins a turn). Each trait is shown on the dossier. Empire-wide effects (invasion strength up to +100%, union odds up to +80%, conquest coins up to +200%, sealift capacity up to +200%, and coins per turn) add up across every nation you control. Nation-only effects (defence, resistance to diplomatic union, garrison recovery and regrowth, revolt risk, weapon access discount) apply to that country, so a target's defensive trait makes it harder to take. The traits are a first draft and are listed in `js/traits.js`.

**Technology.** Each nation in your empire can research four tracks with coins from its dossier, on the same 1–100 scale: level 1 gives 10% of the track's full effect, rising linearly to 100% at level 90, and levels 91–100 add +1% each (up to 110%). Full effects are: weapons systems +50% combat power for that nation's units, logistics & sealift +100% sealift capacity and regrowth, industry 2% of its GDP in $bn as coins per turn (at least 10), and governance +5 points of garrison recovery per turn and −75% revolt risk. A track's full price is 12 × (40 + 5% of the nation's GDP in $bn) coins, priced per level as for access levels.

**Reset.** The Reset button in the top bar erases everything the game has saved: your campaign, coins, purchases, armies, research and redeemed promo codes. It starts a fresh campaign with new random nations. Click it twice to confirm.

**Enemy counter-attacks.** Countries strike back. Invading a country makes it hostile for 12 turns, and it may send its own army at one of your nations it borders, or by sea if it has a navy. Its army is sized at roughly 0.9–2× what defends the target. Treaty allies stay out of it for now. After turn 10, a neighbour more than twice as strong as one of your border nations may also attack unprovoked now and then. Enemy armies travel over turns (red dashed routes) and fight your defenders one round per turn: the accessible share of that nation's forces, its order and any of your armies stationed there. If they win, you lose the nation, and losing your home nation moves your capital to your largest remaining nation. If you have none left, the campaign is lost. Beating off an attack pays some coins in captured equipment.

**Wars between AI countries.** The rest of the world fights too. From turn 3, long-running rivals (Russia–Ukraine, India–Pakistan, Armenia–Azerbaijan, Ethiopia–Eritrea, Sudan–South Sudan, the two Koreas, China–Taiwan, Israel–Iran and others) go to war now and then. Elsewhere a much stronger neighbour may pick on a weak one, though not a treaty ally and not between two stable, well-liked countries. At most six AI wars run at once. They use the same armies and turn-by-turn battles as yours (purple routes and markers on the map), and a country falls when 80% of its forces are destroyed. Conquered countries are shaded purple on the Campaign map, and you can invade them to take them yourself. The Campaign tab lists every world conflict.

**Military profile.** Every country rates 0–10 on patriotism, training, combat experience, logistics, leadership and corruption. About 90 are researched in `js/techlevels.js`, and the rest are estimated from wealth and recent conflicts. Training, experience and leadership raise every unit's skill and corruption lowers it. Patriotism strengthens defence, and logistics decides how well an army fights away from home (overland or, more so, across the sea). Russia, for example, has combat experience but high corruption and middling training, for a unit skill of ×0.86. The US is ×1.09.

**Fronts.** An invasion doesn't flip a country in one go. The ground the attacker holds is drawn as a ragged wave spreading from where it crossed in (the border or coast point nearest the attacker) across the country, with a marching front line at its edge. It advances as the defenders are worn down: gold for your invasions, red for attacks on you and purple for wars between other countries. A nation of yours that is being invaded loses income in proportion to the ground the enemy holds.

**Satellites.** `js/space.js` lists each country's satellites in orbit in about 2025. Government and military satellites are split into reconnaissance, signals intelligence, communications, navigation and early warning, with anti-satellite capability rated 0–3. Private constellations are listed under their home country: Starlink and Starshield, Kuiper, Planet, Maxar, BlackSky, Iridium and others for the US; OneWeb for the UK and France; Jilin-1, Qianfan and Guowang for China; ICEYE for Finland; and so on. They give a combat bonus of up to about +25% (×1.21 for the US in peacetime, ×1.12 for Russia at war). Private satellites count for 30% in peacetime and in full once their country is at war, when they are pressed into national service. With a country selected, a sample of its satellites orbits on the map: government in cyan, private in white, mobilised in gold. The 🛰 button toggles them.

**Intelligence.** Every country is rated 0–10 for human, signals, imagery and cyber intelligence and for counter-intelligence. The ratings come from its secret-assets profile, satellites and intelligence alliances (Five Eyes, NATO), and the dossier lists its main agencies. Your best score sets the fog of war. Enemy strength and units are shown as estimates with an error band (±5% to ±60%) that shrinks with your intelligence and widens with their counter-intelligence. Whether you spot an attack when it launches, or only when it lands as a surprise attack, depends on the same contest.

**Capital ships.** `js/fleet.js` places every aircraft carrier and big-deck amphibious ship in service in 2025, plus fleet flagships, at its home port, shipyard or deployment area. Ships not in service are shown dashed in orange. That includes USS John F. Kennedy (CVN-79), the second Ford-class carrier, fitting out at Newport News for delivery in 2027, as well as carriers in overhaul and Russia's laid-up Admiral Kuznetsov. Selecting a country shows its ships on the map and lists them in the dossier. Each ship card gives class, status, location and approximate capabilities: displacement, length, speed, aircraft carried, crew, propulsion and year. Clicking a ship flies to its pier, where the satellite view often shows the ship itself. Positions at sea are approximate, since deployments change week to week.

**Equipment models.** Each country's inventory is broken down into real weapon models, from Abrams, Leopard 2, T-90M and Type 99 tanks to F-35, Su-34, J-20 and MiG-21 jets, plus each army's standard small arms (M4 and XM7, AK-74M and AK-12, QBZ-191, INSAS and AK-203, and so on). About 30 militaries are listed individually in `js/equipment.js`. The rest use a template that matches where they buy their weapons: Soviet-legacy, NATO, Chinese exports, Western-light, Gulf or non-aligned. The dossier's Equipment section lists the whole inventory by model. Base cards list the models at that base, and hovering a unit icon at base zoom shows its models.

**Technology and readiness.** Every country has a technology generation (1–5, how advanced its designs are) and a readiness (maintenance, spare parts, training). `js/techlevels.js` sets both for about 80 militaries and estimates the rest from GDP per capita. Combat quality is (0.35 + 0.13 × generation) × (0.4 + 0.6 × readiness), so modern designs that are poorly maintained or never fully fielded fight below their paper value. Russia, for example, is Gen 4.2 but only 55% ready, so it fights at about 65% quality against 94% for the US. Readiness also discounts the estimated true strength. On the map, bases and armies are drawn as soldier, tank, jet or ship icons in three tiers: dated (amber-brown: propeller planes, tall old tanks, gunboats), standard (gold) and advanced (glowing cyan: stealth jets, low angular tanks, stealth destroyers). Poorly maintained forces are drawn faded and grey. Researching weapons systems raises a nation's effective generation.

**Promo codes.** The Promo code button in the top bar redeems codes for coins. Each code works once per campaign. The codes are listed in `js/economy.js`.

**Revolts.** Weakly held conquests can rise up, but they don't break away at once. The rebels (15–50% of the country's ground forces, more when order is low) fight its loyal forces and any of your armies stationed there, one round per turn. Their progress bar fills when they win rounds. They gain independence at 100%, which takes about 4–6 turns if you do nothing, and the revolt is crushed once 85% of the rebels are destroyed. Send forces to put it down. Stationed armies cut the chance of a revolt by 70%, and a territory in revolt pays no income. Bought nations never revolt. You win when you control 50% of world GDP.

## Data & methodology

- `js/data.js`: one row per country. Sources: IMF WEO 2025 (GDP), UN WPP (population), SIPRI, IISS Military Balance and national budgets (defence spending), IISS and Global Firepower-style active inventories (equipment), FAS Nuclear Notebook (warheads), and Brand Finance Global Soft Power-style rankings (soft power). All figures are rounded estimates.
- `js/intel.js`: researched "secret asset" profiles for about 80 countries, plus alliances and the mapping of overseas territories to their sovereign states.
- `js/economy.js`: the world asset totals behind the valuation, natural-resource endowments, the weapon catalogue with unit costs, coin prices and promo codes.
- `js/model.js`: the scoring.
  - **Power index**: a square-root-scaled share of the world maximum across 14 weighted capabilities (budget 20%, fighters 10%, troops 8%, then tanks, aircraft, carriers, GDP, artillery, submarines, armoured vehicles, ships, population, helicopters and reserves). Equipment counts are discounted by a quality factor based on GDP per capita. Nuclear-armed states get a deterrent bonus.
  - **Estimated true strength**: the power index recomputed with off-book spending added, multiplied by intelligence, cyber and proxy capability, plus a breakout premium for nuclear-threshold and host states.
  - **Secret-assets index**: a weighted mix of intelligence, cyber, nuclear latency, proxies, hidden spending, gold and sovereign funds.
  - **National value**: the gross value of everything a country owns. First the world's assets are totalled: human capital of $1,620trn (40 years of world labour income at about $59trn a year), real estate and land of $410trn, business equity of $165trn (listed and private companies), infrastructure of $85trn, natural resources in the ground of about $477trn (gross reserve value), gold of about $26trn (central-bank and private holdings) and military hardware of about $3trn. A $70trn cultural and brand pool is shared out by soft power. That puts the **world value at about $2.86 quadrillion**. Each country gets a share of each pool: human capital and infrastructure by GDP, real estate by GDP and population, business equity by GDP weighted toward richer economies, and private gold by GDP and population. Natural resources, central-bank gold and military hardware are counted country by country. For example, the US comes to about $655trn, China $417trn and Russia $118trn. Business equity partly overlaps the property that companies own, so the figure is a gross total, not net wealth. The Vatican is set to $100trn by design. That makes it #5 in the world, and the world value comes to about $2.96 quadrillion.
  - **Soft power in power**: soft power carries a 5% weight in the military power index, standing in for alliances, morale and cultural pull.
  - **Force disposition**: every one of the 196 countries has hand-placed basing: `js/deployments.js` covers 32 major militaries, `js/deployments-more.js` covers Africa and the rest of Europe, and `js/deployments-rest.js` covers everyone else (countries with no army list their police or coast-guard bases). It covers command HQs, corps, division and brigade garrisons, air and naval bases, and forces abroad, at installation or town level from open sources, with sensitive sites such as nuclear storage left out. The US has 412 locations: about 290 installations across the 50 states (every state has at least five, counting National Guard and reserve bases, depots, arsenals and ranges in `js/deployments-us-states.js`), 6 in US territories, and about 222,000 troops at 113 sites in 41 foreign countries. Canada has 14 bases at home. Russia, China, France, the UK, Turkey, India, Germany, Italy and others list their foreign bases too. Forces abroad are absolute personnel numbers. Home locations fill the rest of the active force. Each base lists its weapons by type: troops, tanks, armoured vehicles, artillery, fighter/attack jets, other aircraft, helicopters, naval vessels, submarines and carriers. Each type is shared out by the base's ground, air or naval share, weighted by what the base is (a fighter wing gets the jets, a submarine base the submarines, an armoured division the tanks), and rounded so the bases add up to the national inventory. Every unit a country is known to have, nukes excepted, is assigned to one of its bases at home or abroad. If no base suits a type (helicopters in a force with no army bases, say), it is spread by overall base size. During a campaign the totals are the nation's current home pool, so losses and units sent away show up at the bases. Selecting a country zooms the map to it and loads its states and provinces (Natural Earth admin-1, about 4,600 regions, one small file per country in `vendor/admin1/`). Click a state or province, or choose one from the dossier, to zoom in further and list its bases with their total troops and weapons. Clicking a base, on the map or in a list, zooms the map in to it automatically. At base zoom each base shows a ring of icons, one per kind of unit stationed there, with counts: infantry, tanks, armoured vehicles, artillery, fighter/attack jets, transports and other aircraft, helicopters, ships, submarines and carriers. Hover a circle on the map to see that base's details. Click it to pin a base card and highlight its entry in the dossier list. Clicking a base in the list flies the map to it, and selecting a country zooms the map to it. About 270 named installations were moved to their exact locations using OpenStreetMap (Nominatim) lookups. A match was accepted only when it was a military, airfield or naval feature whose name matched the base and that lay within 40 km of the hand-placed spot. Generic entries such as regional garrisons, frontiers and UN missions keep their hand-placed positions. Only publicly documented sites are mapped: well-known secretive facilities such as Area 51, Tonopah, Pine Gap and Menwith Hill are included, but undisclosed sites are not guessed at. The secret assets score covers hidden strength instead.
  - Countries without a researched profile get a modelled estimate based on their budget and economy. The card labels each profile as either "researched profile" or "modelled estimate".

"Secret assets" are, by definition, not public. These numbers are informed open-source estimates for a game. They are not intelligence.
